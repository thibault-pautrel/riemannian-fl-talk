Manifold

A smooth $d$-manifold is a set covered by charts: homeomorphisms $\varphi : U \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ onto open sets.

Two domains $U$ and $V$, overlapping.

$\varphi$ flattens $U$ into coordinates.

$\psi$ does the same for $V$, with its own coordinates.

On the overlap both descriptions hold, and the transition map $\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}$ is smooth. That is the whole definition.

Say it plainly: curved globally, flat locally. Drag the point to show that it has two sets of coordinates and neither is the point itself.

Tangent space

Take every smooth curve on $\mathcal{M}$ passing through $p$, and collect their velocities at $p$.

$T_p\mathcal{M}=\{\dot\gamma(0)\ :\ \gamma(0)=p\}$, a vector space of dimension $d$.

A first curve through $p$.

A second one, in another direction.

All these velocities fill one plane, attached to $p$.

This is the only linearisation of $\mathcal{M}$ we get, and every algorithm will work in it.

Insist that the plane is not a subset of M. It is attached to p, and it changes with p. Drag the point to show that.

Riemannian metric

A metric is an inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_p$ on each tangent space, varying smoothly with $p$. It gives lengths and angles without ever leaving $\mathcal{M}$.

Here $\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is a submanifold, so the ambient product is inherited.

Take a function $\bar f$ defined around $\mathcal{M}$, and its ambient gradient at $p$.

Project it onto the tangent space:

$\operatorname{grad} f(p)=\Pi_{T_p\mathcal{M}}\bigl(\nabla\bar f(p)\bigr)$
The projection picture is the one to remember. Every Riemannian gradient in this talk is that projection, on Stiefel or on SPD.

Geodesics

How do we join two points of $\mathcal{M}$ while staying on $\mathcal{M}$, and as short as possible?

The straight segment of $\mathbb{R}^n$ leaves the manifold at once.

A geodesic is a curve with no tangential acceleration:

$\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0$, locally length-minimising.

Its speed is constant, and it is longer than the chord. That gap is the curvature of $\mathcal{M}$.

Drag q around to show the two numbers moving. The chord is always shorter and always off the surface.

Exponential map

At $p$, the map $\exp_p$ turns a tangent vector into a point of $\mathcal{M}$.

$\exp_p(v)=\gamma(1)$, where $\gamma$ is the geodesic with $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\dot\gamma(0)=v$.

Pick a direction and a length in $T_p\mathcal{M}$.

The point $\exp_p(tv)$ walks the geodesic. The arc it covers has length $\lVert v\rVert_p$.

A retraction $R_p$ agrees with $\exp_p$ to first order at $t=0$, and costs far less.

Say that exp is the manifold version of "move along a direction". On the last step, insist that the two curves leave p together and separate later.

Logarithmic map

The inverse question. Given $q$, which tangent vector reaches it?

$\log_p = \exp_p^{-1}$ where it is defined, and $d(p,q)=\lVert\log_p(q)\rVert_p$.

The answer lives in $T_p\mathcal{M}$.

Shooting: start from a guess, see where the geodesic lands, correct.

The vector obtained reads $q$ as a direction and a distance. This is the operation that makes averaging on $\mathcal{M}$ possible.

Say that log has a closed form on the sphere and on SPD, none on Stiefel. That absence is exactly why the aggregation section exists.
